How the register works

The four-layer stack

We've had think tanks that argue, journalists who chase the press release, audits that come 3 years late, and government dashboards that measure their own homework.

We never had a citizen-held, append-only, binary scoreboard that exists before the outcome.

That's the missing layer.

Think of the stack:

  1. Rooms: where announcements happen
  2. Process: where files live forever
  3. [MISSING]: where a public test with a name, date, and PASS/FAILED is written down on day one
  4. Accountability: where voters, lenders, courts can actually use it

For 10 years we tried to jump from 2 to 4 and wondered why nothing closed. You can't hold power accountable for a test that was never written down.

The register doesn't need to win the argument about Kamloops, or LNG, or immigration levels, or dual-use with China. It just needs to force:

"Here's what you said would count. Here's who you said owns it. Here's when we will check a primary source. If it's not there, it's FAILED, and here's the layer that blocked it."

Once that's public and fingerprinted, three things change that didn't before:

  • A journalist can't just rewrite the wire report: they have to show the Order, the Gazette, the filing
  • An MP can't say "we're progressing": the follow-up row is already LIVE with the next real step (second financing, C1-C20261025; zero advisories, WATER-ZERO-LTDWA-2027; parcel list, BC-PARCELS-2027)
  • A second lender can read the risk in one place: the title map (C5/FA8/TM1, TITLE-MAP), fee-simple certainty (CL-FEESIMPLE) and corridor signatures (C1-C20261025) are tested on one page

It's not the house. It's the foundation no one poured.

As the last line of The unclosed file puts it:

"...until someone starts writing down the names and the dates."

That's what this is.

The register is a ledger of public commitments and whether they were kept by the date they were tested. This page sets out how a promise gets in, how its test is set, how its scoring date is chosen, how hard cases are ruled, and how the register relates to the desk's essays.

(a) Entry rule

A promise qualifies when all three hold:

  1. It is announced by a minister or the Prime Minister's Office.
  2. It carries a public commitment with a figure or a date.
  3. It falls within a stated scope of the register. The Canadian files cover commitments by Canadian governments. A commitment by a non-Canadian actor (for example a UK public body) is never entered as a Canadian file: where the desk tracks it in public, it sits under Outside files we watch, labelled with its owner, and some are kept off the public register altogether.

Every row records why it qualified.

Each quarter the desk also adds a sample of routine commitments from outside its usual themes, including ones that look likely to pass. This is a check on the desk's own selection: if the register only held promises the desk expected to fail, its failure count would say more about the desk than about the government.

The register is not a census of all promises. It is a record of the commitments the desk chose to test, under the rule above, and every score should be read that way.

(b) Test design

Each new test carries a short note explaining why the bar sits where it does.

Each new test also carries a looser and a tighter variant. Only the main test is the score. The two variants are scored for information only, so a reader can see how much the result depends on where the bar was set.

A row that fails the main test but passes the looser one says so on the row.

(c) Scoring dates

The scoring date is chosen in this order:

  1. The government's own date, if it gave one.
  2. Otherwise, a date set in statute.
  3. Otherwise, 12 months from the announcement.

Any exception to that order is justified in writing on the row.

Where the dates come from now. Of the 349 rows in the register file on 1 Nov 2026, 198 (56.7 per cent) carry a date the desk set, 58 (16.6 per cent) a date the government set, and 17 (4.9 per cent) a date set in statute or another legal deadline. A further 71 (20.3 per cent) carry a date set by another outside source (a court docket, a scheduled release or event, or a company's own timetable), and 5 (1.4 per cent) carry a label that does not fall into these groups. These counts come from each row's date source label. The register does not yet record whether a desk-set date followed the 12-month default or was an exception; that split is not yet recorded and will be from the first rows entered under this rule.

(d) Precedents

When a case falls between the rules, the desk makes a ruling. Each ruling is logged once, in a public precedents log, with its date and the rows it was first applied to, and is applied the same way every time after.

A change to a ruling goes in as a new dated entry. It is never applied quietly to old rows: rows already scored keep the score they were given under the ruling in force at the time, and any later view is added as a dated note.

(e) Ledger versus essays

The register records the scores. The essays argue the desk's reading of them.

The scores stand without the essays. A reader who disagrees with every essay the desk has written can still check each row against its own test, its own date and its own primary source.
